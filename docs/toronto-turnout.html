Place & Politics in Toronto - Part 8

One city, uneven participation

Aniket Kali & Kaiwen Yang

October 26, 2026

Political participation is not evenly distributed across the City of Toronto. Voters in some neighbourhoods come out in greater numbers, while turnout is lower in others.

We mapped voter turnout in Toronto's 2023 mayoral by-election, 2025 provincial election, and 2025 federal election. Turnout differs from election to election, but broad geographic patterns recur across all three.

What sits underneath the turnout map?

Neighbourhood-level data cannot tell us why particular people chose to vote or not to vote. But we know neighbourhood characteristics also do not vary independently: education overlaps with occupation, housing with income, immigration with age and language, and so on – and many of these characteristics are correlated to voting patterns.

To examine these relationships together, we used Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression with 70 measures of neighbourhood population, housing, employment, transportation, and accessibility. In simple terms, PLS finds a small number of components that capture characteristics that tend to occur together and are also related to voter turnout. Each neighbourhood census tract receives a score on each component, showing where it lies relative to other tracts.

The first component is much more closely associated with turnout than the others. Its higher-scoring side includes neighbourhoods with more university education, professional employment, working from home, higher employment rates, and tends to be more centrally located. Its lower-scoring side combines more trades, service and manufacturing work, long commutes, unemployment, larger and multigenerational households, lone-parent families, and larger immigrant and first-generation populations.

The important finding is the combination, not any one characteristic. The data and the model cannot tell us, for example, that immigrants living in a lower-turnout tract are the residents who did not vote. Nor can immigrant share, occupation, household structure or any other individual measure be isolated from the component and treated as a cause of lower turnout.

That distinction matters here. Our turnout denominator includes only Canadian citizens aged 18 and older, so neighbourhoods are not assigned lower turnout simply because they contain more residents who are ineligible to vote. Immigrant and first-generation shares instead describe the wider neighbourhoods in which eligible voters live, alongside differences in employment, education, household structure, commuting and geography.

The broader turnout literature also shows why these characteristics should be interpreted together. A major meta-analysis by Benny Geys reviewed 83 aggregate-level studies of turnout, while a later update by João Cancela and Geys expanded that evidence base to 185 studies and broadly confirmed the earlier findings. Both emphasize that turnout reflects a mix of socioeconomic, political and institutional conditions rather than a single demographic explanation.

Existing Canadian research gives us additional reason to resist treating immigration or racialization as simple explanations for voter turnout. A 2023 study by Stephen White, using Canadian election studies spanning 45 years, found that an apparent relationship between immigrants' length of residence and turnout largely disappeared once the historical period in which different cohorts arrived was taken into account. Even seemingly straightforward demographic relationships can therefore reflect political socialization and historical context rather than fixed characteristics of a group.

Likewise, recent Canadian research on racialized voters finds differences in turnout between several racialized groups and white Canadians, but also finds that socioeconomic circumstances and political engagement explain part of those gaps, while a substantial portion remains unexplained. Other work by Statistics Canada cautions us to note that many racialized Canadians still participate in politics beyond the ballot box: by engaging with news, going to meetings or demonstrations, signing a petition, or wearing signs in support of a cause.

The component is therefore better understood as a wider social geography involving class, employment, settlement, household responsibilities, commuting, and location. Across the 582 tracts with reliable data, its score correlates 0.72 with mean turnout, substantially more strongly than the other components identified by the model.

This component is not peculiar to one election. When we repeat the analysis separately for municipal, provincial and federal turnout, essentially the same underlying components reappear. Neighbourhood characteristics predict municipal turnout more strongly, but the broad pattern remains very similar across all three levels.

The model also identifies other recognizable components of Toronto's geography: relatively settled family neighbourhoods versus younger and more mobile renter areas; housing need and apartment living versus higher-income family ownership; and a weaker housing and employment contrast. These components are all much less closely related to turnout than the first.

Making electoral participation easier

None of this tells us that a particular policy would increase turnout. It does suggest a different way of framing the problem: lower participation may reflect barriers that institutions can reduce, rather than a lack of interest within particular neighbourhoods.

Time and convenience are part of that picture. In an Elections Canada survey following the 2025 federal election, 34% of voters said they chose their voting method because the date and time worked with their schedule, while 28% cited having a nearby voting location. Among non-voters, 24% cited reasons related to the electoral process, and 23% cited everyday-life or health reasons.

Access matters too. Observational research using Ontario polling locations found that greater distance or travel time to the polls was generally associated with lower turnout. And in the 2025 federal election, 44.2% of ballots were cast through advance or other alternative voting methods, showing how widely voters already use more flexible options.

Taken together, polling-place location, advance voting, voting hours, transportation, work schedules, and caregiving are useful areas for further study. Our model does not directly measure most of these mechanisms, so they are better treated as plausible ways to make participation easier, not as effects demonstrated by this analysis.

Data and methods

This analysis combines results from Toronto's 2023 mayoral election, the 2025 Ontario provincial election, and the 2025 federal election with 2021 Census data, the 2022/23 Transportation Tomorrow Survey, and Statistics Canada's 2021 Proximity Measures Database.

Because each election uses different polling boundaries, we use population-weighted spatial interpolation to estimate results for Toronto's 585 census tracts (2021 boundaries). Turnout is measured as votes cast relative to the estimated number of Canadian citizens aged 18 and older in the tract, providing a consistent population-based measure across all three elections. Our main outcome variable in the model is average turnout across the three elections.

We then use 70 neighbourhood measures covering demographics, household structure, education, income and employment, occupation, housing, immigration and citizenship, language, transportation, urban form and access to services. Because many overlap, we use Partial Least Squares regression (PLS) to identify combinations of characteristics that move together and are also related to turnout. Cross-validation selects four components, with most of the turnout relationship concentrated in the first.

We test that result in several ways. The main component remains very similar when each election is modelled separately, when the predictor set is reduced, and when transportation and broader spatial-context variables are removed. We also compare PLS with Principal Component Regression, supervised PCA and Elastic Net, and use spatial cross-validation and bootstrap resampling to test whether the result is sensitive to geography or the observed sample.

The main pattern remains stable across these checks, but the analysis is descriptive and predictive rather than causal. Election results are spatially estimated, the denominator comes from the 2021 Census, and neighbourhood-level relationships cannot identify individual behaviour. The components are continuous summaries of overlapping neighbourhood characteristics, not fixed types of people or places.

A more detailed technical write-up describes the interpolation, variables, model selection, robustness checks and limitations in full, and the analysis notebooks document each step: vote interpolation, predictor selection, PLS model, robustness checks, and final synthesis.
