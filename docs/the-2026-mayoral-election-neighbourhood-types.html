Place & Politics in Toronto - Part 10

The 2026 mayoral election: What kinds of neighbourhoods voted for whom?

Zack Taylor, Aniket Kali, and Jeff Allen

November 10, 2026

Chow and Bradford appealed to different kinds of voters. The Star’s Vote Compass project found that people who favoured one or the other candidate seemed to live in “different cities”, with Chow supporters being most concerned about housing and transit, while Bradford’s reported property tax rates and crime and policing as their most important issues. As our social geography correlation tool shows, this variation is reflected in their geographic bases of support. Each candidate tended to receive support from different types of neighbourhoods – reflecting deeper patterns across the city.

Transportation behaviour gives a strong hint about what separates neighbourhoods that support Chow and Bradford. Commuting by bicycle or walking, and neighbourhood walkability are among the strongest correlates of neighbourhood support for Chow. By contrast, commuting by car is Bradford’s strongest.

Chow’s neighbourhoods are also disproportionately home to younger, “creative class” types – people who work in arts and culture occupations, are unmarried or living common-law, and rent. Bradford’s neighbourhoods are where “homevoters” live: people who are married, live in single-detached houses, and have higher household incomes. This may be interpreted as something of a class divide. Bradford’s geographic coalition supports a narrative of pocketbook voting: people voting for low taxes and protection of property and property values. The characteristics of Chow’s coalition are consistent with the prioritization of preserving and expanding collective amenities.

Growing the pie

In the previous post we noted that Chow won with a “core-plus” strategy – building a coalition of neighbourhoods from the centre outwards toward the periphery. This was not only the result of a greater number of votes from higher turnout, but also a substantial increase in the percentage of votes for her as well.

Where did all of these new votes come from? Half of them came from census tracts that she won in 2023 largely in Old Toronto, East York, and northern Scarborough. But a substantial number also came from parts of the city that she flipped, largely in the rest of Scarborough and along the Weston Road corridor. Moreover, even places that Bailão won in 2023 which stuck with Bradford in 2026, she increased her share of the vote from 21.7% to 39.3%.

Who exactly are these new supporters? For the most part, they resemble the same kinds of voters that Chow won in 2023: transit and bicycle riders, renters, young people, and lower income residents. Across the city, her increase in vote share was tied to neighbourhoods with a higher proportion of renters, and this is especially prevalent in Scarborough. In one sense, this isn’t very surprising – Chow has pursued numerous pro-renter policies including a renoviction bylaw, increased support to RentSafeTO, and remedial action at 500 Dawes Road. But in another sense, it reflects the growth of half of the city as a political constituency in its own right, overcoming its historical association with low turnout.

At the same time, some of her weakest gains were in neighbourhoods with a high proportion of visible minority residents. Conversely, this is where Bradford began to make inroads, adhering to a broader trend of an openness to right-wing politics from visible minorities, or plain abstention, which School of Cities research has previously highlighted.

Identity voting?

Toronto is a highly ethnically diverse city, and so we might expect to see ethnic affinity voting for Chow. In 2023, and again in 2026, pundits suggested that as a Chinese woman, Chow would potentially attract support from the city’s large Asian community. In fact we find this to be correct, if weakly. Neighbourhood vote shares for Chow are weakly correlated with neighbourhood Chinese population share in 2014 and 2026, regardless of location. In 2023 there was a stronger correlation in suburban areas, but a weaker one in 2026. Without individual-level evidence from surveys we cannot definitively explain this pattern. Nevertheless, we may conclude that Chinese ethnic affinity voting likely occurs, but it is a marginal factor, and was more present in 2023 than in 2026 or 2014.

In the context of the polarized debate on Israel and Palestine, the media reported criticism from the Jewish community that Chow was insufficiently supportive of them, while others have pushed back on that criticism as relying on anti-Palestinian tropes. Bradford called her out for not showing up to a Walk for Israel and a debate on antisemitism, but also came under criticism for courting Islamophobes.

Do we see any evidence of Jewish neighbourhoods disproportionately supporting Bradford or Muslim neighbourhoods disproportionately supporting Chow? Again, we have weak evidence that this is the case. As the proportion of the neighbourhood population that is Jewish increases, that neighbourhood was more likely to support Bradford over Chow. The corresponding trend for neighbourhoods by Muslim population is much weaker.

Plus ça change…

In the end, the geography of the 2026 election, along with the limited polling data reported in the media, suggests that the contest hinged on divergent neighbourhood interests rather than on other potential divides, including ethno-racial group conflict. The 2026 election is not unique. With the three-way 2014 election being the principal exception to the trend, we find that transportation behaviour and housing type and tenure, which are in turn related to distance from City Hall, are powerful positive or negative correlates of support for winning mayoral candidates across time. Toronto politics is to a large degree a politics of mobility and location – where people live, the nature of their relationship to housing, and how they move through the city.

Data and methods

All data are for Toronto’s 585 census tracts, on 2021 boundaries. Official election results and the voting subdivisions (poll catchment areas) were downloaded from the City of Toronto, and election results and census characteristics were each apportioned to these tracts. Everything here describes neighbourhoods, not how individual voters, or any group of people, voted. The code and data used to build this page and its graphics are on GitHub.

  • Elections: poll-level mayoral results, 1997 to 2026, split into dissemination blocks by population and summed to 2021 census tracts. Only election-day votes are included, as advance and mail-in votes can’t be placed in a neighbourhood. The map and the votes Chow added use the polls that can be mapped, about 78% of valid votes in 2023 and 76% in 2026, so the 2023 baseline is not the official result, in which Chow led Ana Bailão 37.2% to 32.5%.
  • Census: Statistics Canada’s 2021 Census of Population, including median age. The chart of the winners’ vote over time uses the census nearest each election, 1996 to 2021; data from 1996 to 2016 were apportioned to 2021 census tracts using the Canadian Longitudinal Census Tract Database (Allen and Taylor, 2018).
  • Correlations: Pearson coefficients across tracts, unweighted, between a candidate’s share of valid election-day votes and a tract characteristic.
  • Boundaries: census tracts from Statistics Canada and former municipalities from the City of Toronto, as in other posts in this series.
